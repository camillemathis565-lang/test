Policy review · France's land borders · September 2026

Small shops on France's borders: what exists, what is missing, what to do

An inventory of EU, national and local measures that reach retail SMEs in French border zones, read against a spatial model of cross-border spending along all eight land borders. It compares France with eight other borders with large excise gaps, and ends with nine recommendations for the French government.

Evidence: 40+ official sources (European Commission, Légifrance, Ministry of the Economy / DGE, ANCT, DGDDI, INSEE, OFDT, SDES, Parliament, CCIs, cross-border observatories). Model: Huff gravity model on 317,000 OpenStreetMap shops and 25 million border residents, with tobacco calibrated on the 2020 border closure.

In short

  • France's policy stack does not see its borders. None of the big retail and town-centre programmes uses exposure to neighbouring countries as a criterion. That includes Action Cœur de Ville, Petites Villes de Demain, the 2025 "Lever le rideau" plan, France Ruralités Revitalisation zones and the commercial-zone transformation plan. Border instruments exist, but they deal with governance (the Aachen Treaty committee, the 3DS law, BRIDGEforEU), with enforcement (tobacco import rules) or with tobacconists only.
  • The model shows the flows are large and uneven. Border-strip residents spend about €6.1bn a year abroad and receive about €6.0bn. France loses on everyday goods, where prices drive trips (Germany −€0.84bn net, Spain −€0.45bn). It gains on comparison goods (Switzerland +€0.62bn, Belgium +€0.38bn). Peripheral food retail and tobacconists absorb most of the losses.
  • Tobacco is the sharpest case. Calibrated on the 2020 closure, the model puts about 9% of French cigarette consumption, roughly €1.7bn a year, in neighbouring countries. Closing borders would lift tobacconists' sales by 50–60% in the Pyrénées-Orientales, Ariège and Moselle. Current support pays a flat €2,500 diversification bonus in border départements.
  • Compared with Europe, France is two-way on everyday retail but a net importer of excise goods. Unlike Norway, Denmark or Switzerland, whose residents only shop outwards, French border retail roughly balances (€6.1bn out, €6.0bn in), and France earns on tourism. On tobacco, 11.9% of cigarettes consumed were bought legally abroad against 1.3% of French sales going to foreigners, plus the largest illicit share in Europe (KPMG 2024).
  • Other borders show what works. Norway, Denmark, Ireland and Germany measure their leakage officially. Denmark, Norway and Finland cut taxes to stem it, at a public-health cost (Finnish alcohol deaths rose sharply). German leakage to Czechia halved when Czech prices rose. US studies find the same price response (elasticity 3.1) as the French tobacco calibration.
  • Main recommendations. Build a border-exposure index and use it to target existing programmes. Create an official measure of cross-border retail flows. Make tobacconist support proportional to exposure. Require cross-border impact assessment in commercial planning. Capture inflows better (tax-free shopping, cross-border marketing). Put proximity retail into the next Interreg generation. Add a trigger for sudden price or currency shocks.

Who carries the loss. Once losses are netted against gains, French independents are roughly at break-even overall and chains lose about 0.6%. The split that matters is by product. Everyday goods lose everywhere, worst for peripheral zones (−3.9% for chains, −4.6% for independents). Comparison goods gain everywhere (+2.9% to +4.6%). Tobacconists, who are almost all independents, are the clear exception. Fuel could not be calibrated with public data.

What this means for France.

France is not a Norway or a Switzerland. Its high prices don't push spending out across the board: on everyday retail the border works in both directions, and the country gains on tourism. The weakness is concentrated in excise goods. France is among Europe's largest net importers of legally bought cigarettes (behind Norway, the Netherlands, Ireland and Germany in relative terms), has the largest illicit market, and faces the biggest exporting neighbours: Spain sends 10% of its legal sales abroad, and Luxembourg 88%. Policy should therefore target excise-exposed towns and products (R1, R3, R8) rather than general retail competitiveness, while the tourism and comparison-goods surplus is protected and cultivated (R5).

7 · Limits

How far to trust this

  • General retail flows come from an uncalibrated model. The directions and rankings are more reliable than the euro amounts. Tobacco is calibrated on 12 observed 2020 figures but misses Moselle (+53% modelled vs +26% observed, likely because cross-border commuters kept buying in Luxembourg during the closure). Fuel could not be identified from annual data.
  • OpenStreetMap coverage and brand tagging vary by country, which biases the chain versus independent split in Spain and Italy.
  • The inventory covers official sources available in September 2026. Local initiatives are sampled (CCIs, Eurodistricts, observatories), not exhaustive. Some programme details (Interreg ALCOTRA, POCTEFA) were not fully verified from programme documents.
